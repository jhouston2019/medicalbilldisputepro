Billed After Insurance Already Paid: What to Check and What to Do

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Billing errors
Short answerIf a provider bills you after your insurer paid, compare the bill with your Explanation of Benefits. For in-network care, what you owe is generally the patient responsibility amount on the EOB, not the provider's original charge. If the bill is higher, ask billing whether the insurance payment was posted and request a corrected statement.

Why would a provider bill you after insurance paid?

Often the timing is to blame. A provider may send a statement before the insurer finishes processing the claim, or the insurer's payment may arrive but not get posted to your account. Other causes include a secondary insurance that was never billed, a claim reprocessed or denied later, or a payment that went to you instead of the provider.

Sometimes the bill is correct because you still owe a deductible, copay, or coinsurance. The EOB will tell you which situation you are in.

How do you compare the EOB to the bill?

Remember that an EOB is from your insurer and is not a bill. Put the EOB and the provider's statement side by side and match the date of service and claim details.

  • Patient responsibility on the EOB versus the balance due on the provider's bill
  • The amount the plan paid, and whether the provider's statement shows a matching payment
  • The allowed amount and any contractual adjustment the provider agreed to write off
  • Whether the provider is listed as in-network or out-of-network
  • Claim number, dates of service, and the services listed

What should you say when you call billing?

Keep it simple and factual. Say: my EOB shows my plan paid one amount and my patient responsibility is a lower figure, but your statement asks for more. Can you tell me whether the insurance payment and adjustment were posted? Ask them to send a corrected statement, and note their name, the date, and any reference number.

If they say the insurer has not paid, ask for the claim number they submitted and the date, so you can check it with your insurer.

When should you contact your insurer?

Call member services if the provider says the claim was denied, if the EOB and the provider disagree about what was paid, or if the claim was reprocessed. Ask what the claim status is, what the allowed amount was, and whether the provider is permitted to bill you the difference.

If the claim was denied and you want to challenge it, you can appeal through your plan and may request an independent external review. The deadline is printed on your denial notice or EOB; many plans allow roughly six months, but check yours.

How do you dispute the bill in writing?

Write to the billing department with the account number, date of service, EOB details, and the amount you believe is correct. Ask for a corrected statement, a hold on collections while the issue is reviewed, and a response within 10 business days. Attach a copy of the EOB, and keep the original.

If you have already paid more than the EOB says you owe, ask for a refund or credit and include proof of payment.

What if the bill is actually correct?

Frequently asked questions

Should I pay a bill that is higher than my EOB says I owe?

Generally not until the difference is explained. Ask billing to reconcile the statement with your EOB and send a corrected one. If part of the bill matches the patient responsibility, you may pay that part and dispute the rest, but ask the provider whether that is accepted.

What does patient responsibility mean on an EOB?

Patient responsibility is the amount your insurer says you owe the provider, typically your deductible, copay, or coinsurance for that claim. It is not a bill by itself. Compare it with the provider's statement to see whether they match.

Why did my insurance pay the doctor but I still got a bill?

The plan may have paid only part of the allowed amount, leaving a deductible, copay, or coinsurance for you. The bill may also come from a different provider, such as a lab or radiologist. Check the EOB for patient responsibility and the provider name on each claim.

Can a bill sent after insurance paid go to collections?

It can if it stays unpaid and unresolved, so respond promptly in writing and ask that the account be held while you dispute it. If a collector contacts you, you can dispute the debt in writing within 30 days of receiving its validation notice, and keep copies of everything.

What if my insurer paid me instead of the provider?

That can happen, particularly with out-of-network care. Check the EOB for who received the payment. Ask your insurer to explain and ask the provider how they want to handle it. Keep any payment you received separate and documented until it is resolved.
