How to dispute a laboratory or diagnostic bill

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Billing situations
Short answerTo dispute a lab bill, request an itemized statement, compare it with your EOB, and look for duplicate tests, repeated blood-draw fees and an out-of-network lab you did not choose. Then ask the lab and your insurer to correct the claim, and put your dispute in writing.

Why does a lab send its own bill?

When your doctor orders blood work, the sample may go to an outside laboratory. That lab bills your insurer, and then bills you for whatever the insurer does not cover. You may never have chosen the lab or seen its name, which is why the statement can arrive as a surprise.

Hospital labs, independent labs and specialty diagnostic companies all bill differently. The first task is to learn exactly which company sent the bill and whether it is in your plan's network.

What errors are common on lab bills?

Read the itemized list against the tests your doctor actually ordered.

  • The same test or panel listed twice for one collection date.
  • Individual tests billed separately when they belong to a panel your doctor ordered as a group.
  • A routine blood draw (venipuncture, code 36415) billed more than once for one visit.
  • Tests you do not remember being ordered.
  • A wrong date or a wrong patient name.
  • A balance that remains after your insurer already paid.

What if the lab is out-of-network?

If your doctor's office sent your sample to a lab outside your plan's network, the insurer may pay little or nothing. Ask your doctor's office which lab it used and why, and ask your insurer how the claim was processed.

Federal No Surprises Act protections for out-of-network providers at in-network facilities cover specific settings, such as clinicians working at an in-network hospital. An independent lab that is not located at a facility may fall outside them, so ask your insurer and check cms.gov/nosurprises rather than assuming the rule applies.

How do you work the dispute?

Call the lab's billing department and ask for an itemized bill, the name of the ordering doctor, the date of collection and a copy of the claim it sent to your insurer. Ask whether the account can be paused while it is reviewed.

Then call your insurer and ask whether it processed the claim correctly and whether a corrected claim should be filed. If your doctor's office ordered a test by mistake, ask the office to contact the lab. Write down every name, date and reference number.

When should you put it in writing?

If calls stall, send a short written dispute listing each charge, the amount and the reason you question it. Many providers and collectors expect disputes within a short window printed on the bill, so act promptly and keep proof of when you wrote.

Frequently asked questions

Why did I get a lab bill when my insurance covers lab work?

Coverage depends on the specific lab and plan terms. The lab may be out-of-network, the claim may have been denied or processed with a deductible, or the bill may include amounts you owe under your plan. Compare the lab's statement to your EOB to see what the insurer actually allowed and listed as your responsibility.

Can I be billed twice for the same blood test?

You should not owe for a duplicate test, but billing systems do create duplicates. If the same test appears twice for one collection date, ask the lab to explain. It may be an error, or it may be a repeat test your doctor ordered. Request an itemized bill and your doctor's order to check.

Who decides which lab processes my sample?

Often your doctor's office does, based on its own agreements. You can usually ask in advance which lab will be used and whether it is in your plan's network. If you did not get that chance, tell the lab and your insurer when you dispute the bill.

Can I get my lab results as part of the dispute?

Patients generally have a right to request their medical records under HIPAA, and providers must generally respond within 30 days. Lab results and billing records that are part of the designated record set may be included. Ask the lab or your doctor's office in writing.

What if the lab sends the bill to collections?

If a third-party debt collector contacts you, you can dispute the debt in writing within 30 days of receiving the validation notice, and the collector must pause collection until it verifies the debt. Respond in writing and keep copies. You can also submit complaints to the CFPB.
