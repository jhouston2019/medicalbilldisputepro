How to Read an Explanation of Benefits (EOB)

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · How-to guides
Short answerAn Explanation of Benefits (EOB) is a statement from your insurer showing what was billed, what the plan allowed and paid, and what you may owe. It is not a bill. Find the patient responsibility amount, then compare it with what the provider is asking you to pay.

What is an EOB, and what is it not?

An EOB comes from your health insurer after it processes a claim. It explains how the plan treated the provider's charges. It does not ask for payment, and you do not send money to the insurer based on it.

The bill comes from the provider. Reading the two together is the single most useful habit for catching billing errors.

Which lines should you find first?

Layouts differ by insurer, but most EOBs include the same core information. Look for these items:

  • Provider name and date of service
  • Amount billed, which is the provider's charge
  • Allowed amount, which is what the plan considers payable
  • Amount the plan paid
  • Deductible, copay, and coinsurance amounts applied
  • Patient responsibility, the amount you may owe
  • Any denial or adjustment remarks

What does a simple example look like?

For illustration only, imagine a provider billed $1,000, the plan allowed $600, the plan paid $500, and the EOB lists $100 as your responsibility. If the provider's statement then asks you for $500, the gap between $100 and $500 deserves a call to the billing office.

Not every difference is an error. Out-of-network rules, deductibles that reset, or a second claim can change the numbers, which is why you ask questions instead of assuming.

What mismatches should you watch for?

Put the EOB and the provider's itemized bill next to each other and check these points:

  • The provider's balance is larger than the patient responsibility
  • The bill includes a service or date that is not on the EOB
  • The EOB shows a denial but the provider is billing you the full charge
  • You received two EOBs for the same service
  • The provider shows no insurance payment although the EOB shows one

What if you have Medicare?

Medicare sends a Medicare Summary Notice (MSN) instead of an EOB. It is also not a bill. Read it the same way and compare it with the provider's statement. Medicare appeals exist, and the deadline is on your notice.

What should you do when the numbers do not match?

Call the provider first if their bill disagrees with the EOB. Call your insurer if you think the claim was processed incorrectly. If a claim was denied, the appeal deadline is printed on your denial notice or EOB. Many plans allow roughly six months, but check yours.

Step-by-step

  1. Gather both documentsCollect the EOB and the provider's itemized bill for the same date of service.
  2. Confirm the basicsCheck that the name, date of service, and provider on both documents match your visit.
  3. Find patient responsibilityLocate the amount the EOB says you may owe, including deductible, copay, and coinsurance.
  4. Compare billed and allowed amountsNote the difference and look for any explanation printed on the EOB.
  5. Match it to the bill balanceSee whether the provider's requested amount equals the patient responsibility.
  6. Note any denialsRead the remarks for denied lines and record the reason given.
  7. Call to resolve differencesContact the provider or insurer, whichever appears responsible, and log what you are told.

Frequently asked questions

Do I pay the amount listed on my EOB?

No. An EOB is not a bill, and you pay the provider, not the insurer, for your share. Wait until the provider's bill arrives, then compare it with the patient responsibility on the EOB. If the two disagree, ask the billing office to explain the difference before paying.

Why is the allowed amount lower than the billed amount?

The billed amount is the provider's list charge. The allowed amount is what your plan treats as payable under its terms. For in-network providers, the difference is often an adjustment rather than something you owe. Out-of-network situations differ, so check your plan documents and ask the insurer.

What should I do if my EOB says a claim was denied?

Read the reason code or remark, then look at your plan's appeal instructions. You can usually appeal through the plan's internal process and may later request independent external review. The deadline is printed on the denial notice or EOB, so note it right away.

What if the provider bills me before the EOB arrives?

Wait for the EOB before paying, if you can. Contact the billing office, tell them you are waiting for your insurer's processing, and ask them to hold the account. If the due date is close, ask in writing for more time.
