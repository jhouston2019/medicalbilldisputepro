How to Dispute a Medical Bill, Step by Step

What does it mean to dispute a medical bill?

Disputing a bill means asking the provider to review a charge you believe is wrong and correct it. The problem might be a clerical mistake, a service you did not receive, a charge your insurer already paid, or an amount that does not match your Explanation of Benefits (EOB). Most disputes start with a phone call and end with a written request.

You do not need legal language or proof of fraud. You need documents that show where the bill and the records disagree.

What should you gather before you call?

A few minutes of preparation makes the call far more productive. The goal is to have the bill and the EOB side by side before anyone asks you to pay.

  • The itemized bill, not just the summary statement
  • Your EOB from the insurer for the same date of service
  • Your account number, date of service, and the billing office phone number
  • Any estimate, authorization, or discharge paperwork you received
  • A notebook or folder for call notes

What should you look for when comparing the documents?

Mark each questionable item and write one line about why it looks wrong. A $400 charge that appears twice on the same date is a clear example worth circling.

  • The same service listed twice on one date
  • Quantities or units that seem too high
  • Dates when you were not at the facility
  • Supplies or services you do not remember receiving
  • A balance larger than the patient responsibility on the EOB
  • Charges for care your insurer denied or never received

What should you say on the phone?

Be calm and specific. Give your account number, name the line item, and say what you think is wrong: 'This charge appears twice on March 3. Can you review it and correct the bill?' Write down the person's name, the date and time, and what they promise to do next.

Ask whether the account can be marked as in dispute while the review is underway. Practices vary, so ask for that confirmation in writing.

Why does a written dispute matter?

A phone call leaves no proof. A short letter that lists the account number, the items in question, what you want done, and a response date creates a record. Send it in a way you can track, such as certified mail or a patient-portal message you can screenshot.

Where can you go if the provider does not fix it?

You can complain to your state insurance department about an insurer, or to your state attorney general about provider billing practices. For surprise bills covered by the No Surprises Act, the CMS help desk is 1-800-985-3059. If the bill has moved to a collector, a separate set of rights applies.

Step-by-step

  1. Request the itemized billAsk the provider's billing office for a line-by-line itemized statement, not only the summary balance.
  2. Get your EOBDownload or request the EOB for the same date of service. Remember that the EOB is not a bill.
  3. Compare line by lineMatch each charge to the EOB's patient responsibility and mark anything duplicated, unexplained, or already paid.
  4. Call the billing officeDescribe the specific error, ask for a correction, and record the name, date, and what was promised.
  5. Send a written disputeList the account number, disputed items, and the correction you want, and attach copies of your evidence.
  6. Ask for a holdRequest that the account not be sent to collections while the review is pending, and ask for that in writing.
  7. Escalate if neededIf the provider stalls, use your insurer's appeals process or contact the relevant regulator.

Frequently asked questions

Can I dispute a medical bill if I already paid part of it?

Yes. Keep your receipt, tell the billing office which charges you still dispute, and ask them to apply your payment correctly. Paying part of a bill does not by itself stop you from questioning other charges, but keep proof of what you paid and when, and mention the payment in your written dispute.

How long does a medical bill dispute take?

It varies by provider and by how complicated the error is. Some clerical fixes happen after one call, while others take several rounds of back and forth. Ask the billing office for a timeframe, put your own response date in your letter, and follow up in writing if you do not hear back.

Should I dispute with the insurer or the provider?

Work out where the error is first. If the provider's bill does not match the EOB, start with the provider. If the insurer processed the claim incorrectly or denied it, use the plan's internal appeal and then external review. Many situations involve both, so keep a separate record for each.

Do I need a lawyer to dispute a medical bill?

Most billing errors can be raised without one. A healthcare attorney or billing advocate may help when the amount is large, a lawsuit has been filed, or fraud is alleged.
