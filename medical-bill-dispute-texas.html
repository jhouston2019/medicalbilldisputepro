Disputing a Medical Bill in Texas

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Dispute a bill by state

Step zero: which kind of plan do you have?

Texas is large, and plan types vary widely. Who can help depends heavily on how your coverage is set up, so check this before filing anything.

Plans bought on your own or through a fully insured employer arrangement are generally overseen by the state insurance regulator. Some large employers fund their own plans, and those are generally outside state insurance regulators' reach. Your plan documents or HR department can tell you which you have.

  • Fully insured plan: the Texas Department of Insurance can take complaints against the insurer.
  • Self-funded employer plan: start with the plan administrator and the plan's internal appeal; ask HR who to contact.
  • Medicare or Medicaid: use the Medicare Summary Notice review or the state Medicaid agency instead.

The Texas offices in plain terms

The Texas Department of Insurance handles complaints about insurers and the health plans it regulates. The Texas Attorney General's consumer protection office is a place to raise unfair or deceptive billing practices by providers or collectors. Both publish complaint instructions on their sites, and the NAIC map links to the right insurance department.

Federal rules you can use right now

Whatever your plan, these apply, and none of them depends on a Texas-specific law.

  • No Surprises Act: emergency services and out-of-network providers at in-network facilities are protected for most private plans. Ground ambulance bills are not covered.
  • Good faith estimates: if you are uninsured or self-pay and a bill is $400 or more above the estimate, a federal dispute process exists. Check the CMS site for the filing window.
  • FDCPA: a written dispute to a collector within 30 days of the validation notice can pause collection.
  • HIPAA: you can request billing and medical records.

Texas-specific protections: verify before you rely on them

State law may give Texas residents extra rights, especially around out-of-network billing and mediation. This page does not describe them, because the details change and depend on your plan. Read the Department of Insurance's consumer pages or call its help line to confirm what covers your bill.

A realistic walk-through

You have a fully insured plan and receive a $1,200 bill from an out-of-network radiologist after an in-network hospital stay. Ask for an itemized bill, then check your EOB. If you did not sign a notice-and-consent form, cite the No Surprises Act in writing and ask the provider to re-bill at in-network cost sharing. If nothing changes, file with the Texas Department of Insurance or the CMS help desk.

Frequently asked questions

Who regulates health insurance complaints in Texas?

The Texas Department of Insurance takes complaints about insurers and state-regulated health plans. If your employer funds its own plan, the state regulator may not have authority, so begin with the plan administrator's appeal process and ask your HR department.

Can the Texas Attorney General fix my medical bill?

The Attorney General's consumer protection office can receive complaints about unfair or deceptive practices, including by providers or collectors. It does not act as your personal lawyer or promise a result, so keep your own written record as well.

Does the No Surprises Act cover Texas ambulance rides?

Air ambulance services are covered by the federal law. Ground ambulance services are not. For ground ambulance bills, check state law and your plan terms with the Department of Insurance, appeal through your insurer, and try negotiating directly.

What if I have no insurance in Texas?

Ask for a good faith estimate before scheduled care. If the final bill is $400 or more above it, you may start the federal dispute process. You can also ask the hospital about financial assistance, which nonprofit hospitals must have a written policy for.

How long do I have to dispute a bill in Texas?

There is no single universal federal deadline. Many providers and collectors expect disputes within a short window printed on the bill, so act promptly and keep proof of the date you wrote. Check your denial notice or EOB for appeal deadlines.
