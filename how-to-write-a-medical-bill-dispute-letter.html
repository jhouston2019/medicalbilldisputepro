How to Write a Medical Bill Dispute Letter

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · How-to guides
Short answerA good medical bill dispute letter is short, factual, and specific. Include your account number, the date of service, each disputed charge and why it is wrong, what you want done, and a response date. Attach copies of your EOB and itemized bill, keep the originals, and send it in a trackable way.

What does a dispute letter do?

A letter turns a phone conversation into a record. It tells the billing office exactly which charges you question, what evidence you have, and what you want next. It also shows the date you raised the problem, which can matter if the account is later sent to collections.

What belongs in a strong letter?

Aim for one page. Billing staff read many letters, and a clear one gets routed faster.

  • Your name, contact information, account number, and date of service
  • The provider's billing department name and address
  • One sentence stating what you are disputing
  • A list of each charge, its amount, and why you believe it is wrong
  • The outcome you want, such as correction, removal, an itemized bill, or a hold
  • A reasonable response date and how to reach you
  • A list of the documents you enclosed

What should you leave out?

Keep the letter focused on the bill. Extra detail can slow things down or create problems.

  • Threats, anger, or guesses about anyone's motives
  • Medical history that is not needed to explain the charge
  • Your full Social Security number
  • Original documents, since you should send copies only
  • Statements that you owe the amount, if you are disputing it

When should you cite a law or rule?

Cite a rule only when it clearly applies. For example, the No Surprises Act may be relevant to an out-of-network emergency bill, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act applies to third-party collectors, and the HIPAA right of access supports a records request. If you are not sure a rule fits, describe the problem in plain terms instead.

How should you send it and keep proof?

Use certified mail with a return receipt, or a patient-portal message you can screenshot. Keep a copy of the letter, the enclosures, and the delivery confirmation. Note the response date in your calendar and follow up in writing if it passes without an answer.

Step-by-step

  1. Collect your documentsPull together the itemized bill, EOB, and any notes from calls with the provider or insurer.
  2. List each disputed itemWrite the date, description, and amount of every charge you question, and one line on why.
  3. State what you wantSay whether you are asking for a correction, removal, an itemized bill, or a hold on the account.
  4. Add relevant rules onlyMention a federal protection only if it clearly applies to your situation.
  5. Set a response dateAsk for a written reply by a specific date and say how to reach you.
  6. Send and log itMail it with tracking or use the portal, then save copies and the delivery proof.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a medical bill dispute letter be?

One page is ideal. Open with a single sentence stating the dispute, then list the charges in question with amounts and reasons. Billing staff will move faster on a clear, short letter than a long account of everything that went wrong. Attach your evidence rather than describing all of it in the body.

Should I mail the letter or send it electronically?

If you can, do both. Certified mail gives you a delivery record, and a patient-portal or email message gives you a quick timestamp. Whichever you choose, save a copy of what you sent and the confirmation that it was received.

Who should I address the letter to?

Use the billing or patient accounts department named on your statement. Hospitals often also have a patient advocate or patient relations office, which can help when billing is unresponsive. Include your account number on every page so it is easy to match.

Can I use a template for my letter?

Yes, as long as you customize it with your account details, the specific charges, and the outcome you want. A generic letter with blanks left in will not help you. Review every line before sending, and consider a healthcare attorney or billing advocate for large or complicated cases.
