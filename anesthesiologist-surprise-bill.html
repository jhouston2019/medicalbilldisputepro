A surprise bill from an anesthesiologist or other ancillary provider

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Billing situations
Short answerIf an out-of-network anesthesiologist billed you after a procedure at an in-network hospital, the No Surprises Act generally protects insured patients from balance billing for non-emergency care unless you gave proper notice and consent. Compare the bill with your EOB, ask for a copy of any consent, and contact your insurer.

Why does an anesthesiologist bill separately?

Anesthesiologists usually work for independent practice groups rather than the hospital itself. Even when your surgeon and hospital are in your network, the anesthesia group may have its own contract status and its own billing office. The statement can arrive weeks after surgery and may list a large balance.

The same pattern applies to other ancillary providers, such as assistant surgeons, pathologists and radiologists, who you may not have selected and may not have met.

What protections may apply?

For people with most private health plans, the No Surprises Act protects against balance billing from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities for non-emergency services, unless the patient gave proper notice and consent under the law (42 U.S.C. § 300gg-112). Anesthesiology, radiology, pathology and assistant surgeons at an in-network hospital are typical examples.

If the care was an emergency, a related provision protects against balance billing by out-of-network emergency providers (42 U.S.C. § 300gg-111). Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE, VA and Indian Health Service coverage fall outside this law.

What should you look at first?

Gather the bill, your EOB and any paperwork from before the procedure.

  • Whether your EOB shows the anesthesia claim as out-of-network.
  • What the EOB lists as your responsibility compared with the amount on the bill.
  • Any consent or notice form you signed, and whether it clearly explained out-of-network costs.
  • The date of service and the procedure, to confirm they match your records.
  • The time or units billed, which may look too high for the procedure.

What are the steps?

Call the anesthesia group's billing office. Ask for an itemized bill, a copy of any consent you signed, and confirmation of whether they are billing you under the No Surprises Act. Ask them to place the account on hold while it is reviewed.

Then call your insurer and ask whether the claim was processed under the correct rules. If the insurer treated the claim wrongly, ask it to reprocess. Keep a log of each call with date, name and reference number.

How do you escalate?

If you still receive a bill you believe is not allowed, call the CMS No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059 or visit cms.gov/nosurprises. You can also complain to your state insurance department about the insurer's handling and to your state attorney general about billing practices.

Frequently asked questions

Can an anesthesiologist legally balance bill me?

For non-emergency care at an in-network facility, federal law generally prohibits it unless you received proper notice and gave consent. Rules differ for Medicare, Medicaid and other programs. If you were billed more than your plan's normal cost sharing, ask the provider and your insurer to review it.

Did I agree to out-of-network billing by signing hospital forms?

Not necessarily. Consent must meet the law's notice-and-consent requirements. Ask for a copy of whatever you signed and read it. If you cannot find a clear, separate notice about out-of-network costs, say so in your dispute and ask the provider to confirm its basis for billing you.

Why do I owe anything if my EOB shows the claim was paid?

The EOB shows what your plan allowed, paid and lists as your responsibility, which can include a deductible, copay or coinsurance. If the bill is higher than that responsibility, ask why. An out-of-network provider might be billing the difference, which may or may not be allowed.

What if the anesthesia bill seems too high for the procedure?

Ask for an itemized bill showing the time or units, and compare it with the procedure date and length. You can question the amount even if the provider is allowed to bill. A written request to review the charges, with your EOB attached, is a reasonable first step.

Who can help if the provider ignores me?

Start with a written dispute and keep proof. The CMS No Surprises Help Desk can explain federal protections, your state insurance department handles insurer complaints, and your state attorney general may handle provider billing complaints. If a collector contacts you, you can dispute the debt in writing under the FDCPA.
