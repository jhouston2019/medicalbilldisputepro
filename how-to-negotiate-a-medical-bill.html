How to Negotiate a Medical Bill

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · How-to guides
Short answerTo negotiate a medical bill, first fix any errors, then ask the billing office about financial assistance, a prompt-pay or self-pay discount, and an interest-free payment plan. Make a specific offer you can afford, and get any agreement in writing before you pay, including confirmation of what the account will show afterward.

Why fix errors before you negotiate?

Negotiating a balance that includes a duplicate or an incorrect charge means you might agree to a discount on money you never owed. Review the itemized bill and your EOB first, dispute anything wrong, and only then discuss the remaining amount.

What can you ask for?

Billing offices often have more than one option, but they may not mention them unless you ask. Try these requests, one at a time:

  • A review for financial assistance or charity care
  • A discount for paying promptly or in a lump sum
  • A reduced balance that fits what you can afford
  • A monthly payment plan, ideally without interest
  • A pause on collection activity while you talk
  • Written confirmation of whatever is agreed

What can you say on the call?

Keep it direct and polite: 'I want to resolve this account. I have reviewed the bill and my EOB. Can you tell me about financial assistance, or any discount for paying promptly?' Then pause and listen. Write down the name of the person, the date, and each option offered.

If you can pay a specific amount, state it. A concrete offer is easier to approve than a vague request for help.

What should you ask about payment plans?

A plan can make a balance manageable, but the terms matter.

  • Is there interest or a fee?
  • What happens if I miss a payment?
  • Does the account stay with the provider while I pay?
  • Can I pay it off early without a penalty?
  • Can you send the terms in writing?

How do you make sure the deal is documented?

Before you pay, ask for a written agreement that states the amount, the due date or schedule, and that the remaining balance will be adjusted or written off if that is what you negotiated. Pay by a method that leaves a record, and keep the confirmation.

When might outside help make sense?

If the balance is large, the provider is unresponsive, or a lawsuit is threatened, a billing advocate or healthcare attorney may be worth considering. Medical Bill Dispute Pro is a software tool, not a law firm or advocate, and cannot promise any outcome.

Step-by-step

  1. Check the bill for errorsCompare the itemized bill with your EOB and dispute anything duplicated or incorrect first.
  2. Decide what you can payWork out a lump sum and a monthly amount you can sustain without missing other bills.
  3. Ask about assistanceAsk whether the provider has a financial assistance policy and how to apply.
  4. Ask about discountsAsk whether a prompt-pay, self-pay, or lump-sum discount is available.
  5. Make a specific offerState the amount you can pay and when, and ask whether they can accept it.
  6. Get the agreement in writingRequest written terms before you send money, including what happens to the remaining balance.
  7. Pay as agreed and keep recordsSave receipts and confirmations, and follow up if the account does not reflect the agreement.

Frequently asked questions

Can I negotiate a medical bill if I have insurance?

Yes. After your insurer processes the claim, the remaining patient balance can often still be discussed. Ask about financial assistance, payment plans, or discounts. If your insurer denied part of the claim, consider appealing that decision as well, since a successful appeal may reduce what the provider can ask you to pay.

Will negotiating hurt my credit?

A negotiation by itself is not a credit event, but unpaid accounts sent to collections can matter. Credit bureau policies on medical collections have changed over time, so check current rules at consumerfinance.gov. Ask the provider to hold the account while you talk, and get that in writing.

Should I offer the lowest amount I can think of?

Offer a number you can realistically pay and explain your situation briefly. An unrealistic offer may end the conversation, while a fair, specific offer is easier for the billing office to evaluate. You can ask what flexibility they have and respond to what they say.

Is paying a lump sum better than a payment plan?

It depends on your budget. Some providers offer a discount for paying promptly, while a plan spreads the cost. Ask what each option would cost in total, whether interest applies, and how the account will be marked afterward. Choose the one you can actually keep up with.
