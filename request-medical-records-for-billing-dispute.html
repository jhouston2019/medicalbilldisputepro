How to Request Medical Records for a Billing Dispute

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · How-to guides
Short answerUnder the HIPAA right of access (45 C.F.R. § 164.524), you can request your medical records and, generally, billing records that are part of the designated record set. Send a written request naming the dates and records you want. Providers must generally respond within 30 days.

Why do records matter in a billing dispute?

A bill says what the provider charged. The records show what actually happened. Comparing the two can help you check whether a service was documented, whether a procedure matches the charge, and whether the dates and quantities line up. Records are especially useful when you suspect you were charged for something you did not receive.

What can you ask for?

Be specific about dates and departments so the request is easy to fulfill. Depending on your situation, consider asking for:

  • Itemized billing statements and the account history
  • Visit notes and procedure or operative reports
  • Orders, medication administration records, and supply records
  • The discharge summary
  • Signed consent forms, including any notice-and-consent paperwork for out-of-network care
  • Lab and imaging reports tied to the charges you question

How do you make the request?

Put it in writing and sign it. Include your full name, date of birth, contact details, account number, and the dates of service. Address it to the provider's health information management or medical records department, and ask how they accept requests, since many have a form or portal.

Say how you want the records delivered, such as a portal download or a mailed copy. Ask in advance whether any fee applies.

What response should you expect?

Providers must generally respond within 30 days. Note the date you sent the request and the method. If you do not hear back, send a short written follow-up referencing the original date, and ask for the name of the person handling it.

How do you use the records?

Read the notes alongside your itemized bill and highlight any mismatches: a charge with no matching note, a quantity that differs, or a date that is off. You do not need to interpret clinical details yourself. Instead, raise the mismatch in writing and ask the billing office to review it against the chart.

If you want a clinical question answered, ask the provider or your own clinician.

What if the provider does not respond?

Ask to speak with the provider's privacy officer or patient advocate, and send a follow-up that restates your request. You can also contact your state attorney general's consumer office. Keep every message and note the dates.

Step-by-step

  1. Decide what you needList the dates of service and the types of records that relate to the charges in question.
  2. Write a signed requestInclude your identifying details, account number, dates of service, and the records you want.
  3. Send it to the right departmentAddress it to health information management or medical records, using their form or portal if required.
  4. Record the send dateNote when and how you sent it and keep a copy for your file.
  5. Follow up in writingIf the response window passes, send a short reminder referencing your original request.
  6. Compare records to the billLook for charges without matching documentation, and for date or quantity mismatches.
  7. Raise discrepancies in writingSend the billing office a letter listing each mismatch and the correction you are asking for.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to explain why I want my records?

Generally you do not need to give a reason for a request under the right of access. Keep the request focused on the dates and records you need, and mention that you are reviewing a bill only if it helps the provider find the right documents.

Can I get my records through a patient portal?

Often, yes. Many providers let you download visit summaries and results through a portal. A portal may not include everything, such as detailed billing history, so you may still need to send a written request for the rest.

Will the provider charge me for my records?

Some providers charge limited fees for copies, and others do not. Ask before you submit the request and ask whether an electronic copy costs less. Do not let a fee question delay the request itself.

What if the records seem incomplete?

Write back, list what is missing, and ask the provider to confirm whether more exists. Keep both the original response and your follow-up. If something that should have been documented is absent, raise that with the billing office as part of your dispute.
