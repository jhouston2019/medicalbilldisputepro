EOB vs. Medical Bill: What Each One Is and Which to Pay

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Guides & explainers
Short answerAn EOB, or Explanation of Benefits, comes from your insurer and explains how a claim was processed. It is not a bill. A medical bill or statement comes from the provider and asks for payment. Compare the EOB's patient responsibility to the provider's balance, and pay the provider only when they match.

What is an EOB?

An Explanation of Benefits is a notice from your health insurer. It lists the claim the provider submitted, what the plan allowed, what the plan paid, and what you owe, usually under a heading such as patient responsibility. It often says in bold that it is not a bill.

Because it arrives from the insurer, many people mistake it for a request for payment. Do not send money to your insurer based on an EOB unless it separately tells you that you owe a premium or a refund.

What is a medical bill or statement?

A bill comes from the provider: a hospital, doctor's office, lab or ambulance company. It states a balance and a due date and asks you to pay. It may be a summary with a single total, or an itemized bill that shows each service with a date, description, quantity, code and charge.

Statements may arrive before or after the EOB. If a bill shows up first, you can ask the provider to wait until your insurer has processed the claim.

How do you compare them?

Put the EOB and the bill side by side. The numbers should tell the same story. If they do not, one of them has a mistake, or a step has not happened yet.

  • Match the date of service and provider name on both documents
  • Find the patient responsibility on the EOB and compare it with the balance on the bill
  • Check that the services listed on the bill appear on the EOB
  • Look for amounts written off or adjusted on the EOB that the bill still charges you
  • Note whether the EOB shows the provider as in-network or out-of-network

Which should you pay?

You pay the provider, and only after you have checked that the amount matches your patient responsibility. If the bill asks for more, or for a charge the EOB shows as denied, written off or paid by insurance, dispute it before paying. A written note to the billing office with your EOB attached usually works as a first step.

If the EOB shows a denial, the provider may bill you anyway. In that case, read the denial reason and the appeal instructions. The appeal deadline is printed on your denial notice or EOB, and many plans allow roughly six months, but check yours.

What if the two do not match?

Start with a call to the provider's billing office. Ask whether the claim was submitted to your insurer correctly and whether a corrected claim is needed. Then call the number on the back of your insurance card and ask the same question from their side. Write down names and dates. Frequently asked questions

Do I have to pay what my EOB says?

You generally owe the patient responsibility shown on a correct EOB, to the provider, not the insurer. If you think the insurer made a mistake in processing the claim, you can appeal. The deadline is on your denial notice or EOB.

Why did I get a bill for more than my EOB says?

It could be a clerical error, a bill sent before the insurer finished processing, or balance billing by an out-of-network provider. Ask the billing office to explain the difference in writing, and ask them to check the claim against your EOB.

What if I never received an EOB?

Check your insurer's online portal, where EOBs are often posted, or call the number on your insurance card and ask for a copy. If you are uninsured, you will not have one, and you may have a good faith estimate instead.

Does Medicare send an EOB?

Medicare sends a Medicare Summary Notice, or MSN, which serves a similar purpose. It lists claims and what you may owe. Review it against provider bills, and use medicare.gov for information about claims and appeals.

Is a statement the same as a bill?

People use the terms loosely. A statement is usually a provider's periodic account summary that asks for payment, so it behaves like a bill. Ask for an itemized version if you need to see each charge.
