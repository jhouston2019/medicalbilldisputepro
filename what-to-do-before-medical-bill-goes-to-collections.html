What to Do Before a Medical Bill Goes to Collections

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · How-to guides
Short answerBefore a medical bill goes to collections, contact the billing office, ask for the account to be placed on hold, and put any dispute in writing. Request an itemized bill, ask about financial assistance and payment plans, and keep records of every contact. Acting early gives you more options than waiting.

What are the warning signs?

Accounts rarely go to collections without any notice. Look for statements marked past due or final notice, letters that mention referral to an outside agency, or phone calls from the billing office about an unpaid balance. If you see one of these, treat it as a prompt to act this week, not next month.

What should you do first?

Call the billing office and ask where the account stands. Ask whether it is still with the provider, whether a date has been set for referral, and what they need from you to hold it. Write down the name, date, and answers.

Then follow up in writing so the hold request is on record.

Which requests help most?

You can make several requests in the same call or letter:

  • An itemized bill, so you can check each charge
  • A hold on the account while you review or dispute it
  • A review for financial assistance or charity care
  • A payment plan for any amount you agree you owe
  • Confirmation that your insurer has processed all claims

Why put the dispute in writing now?

A written dispute dated before the account moves shows you raised the problem early. Include your account number, the charges in question, and the outcome you want, and send it with tracking. If you are unsure how to structure it, see the guide on writing a dispute letter.

What changes if it does go to collections?

If a third-party collector takes over, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act applies to that collector, generally not to the original provider collecting its own bill. The collector must send a validation notice, and a written dispute within 30 days of receiving it can require the collector to stop collection until it verifies the debt.

Credit bureau policies on medical collections have changed over time; check current rules at consumerfinance.gov.

Step-by-step

  1. Read every notice carefullyLook for due dates, final-notice language, and any mention of referral to an outside agency.
  2. Call the billing officeAsk where the account stands, when referral might happen, and what they need to hold it.
  3. Request a hold in writingSend a short message asking that the account not be referred while you review it.
  4. Ask for an itemized billGet a line-by-line statement and compare it with your EOB.
  5. Dispute errors in writingList each questioned charge, the reason, and the correction you want.
  6. Ask about assistance and plansRequest a financial assistance review and a payment plan for any undisputed balance.
  7. Document everythingLog each call and save every letter, portal message, and receipt in one folder.

Frequently asked questions

How will I know a bill is about to go to collections?

You may not always get a clear warning. Past-due and final notices are common signs, but the safest approach is to ask the billing office directly where the account stands and whether a referral date has been set. Ask them to note your inquiry and confirm any hold in writing.

Does disputing the bill stop it from going to collections?

Not automatically. A dispute can lead the provider to review the account, but you should also ask in writing for a hold while the review happens. Keep a dated copy of both the dispute and the hold request.

Will a payment plan keep the account out of collections?

It can, if the provider agrees and you keep up with the terms. Ask what happens if you miss a payment, whether interest applies, and whether the arrangement is documented in writing before you make the first payment.

Can I still dispute a bill after it is with a collector?

Yes. Send a written dispute to the collector within the period stated in its validation notice, and keep raising billing errors with the original provider too. Copies of your earlier written disputes can be useful evidence.
