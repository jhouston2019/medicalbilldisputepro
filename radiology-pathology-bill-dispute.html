A separate radiologist or pathologist bill after a hospital visit

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Billing situations
Short answerRadiologists and pathologists often bill separately from the hospital because they read your images or samples as independent physician groups. The bill can be legitimate, but check it against your EOB, confirm network status, and ask whether the No Surprises Act applies if the group is out-of-network at an in-network hospital.

Why does this bill show up separately?

When you have an X-ray, scan or biopsy, two different things happen. The hospital provides the equipment, staff and facility. A physician, usually a radiologist or pathologist, interprets the results. That physician often belongs to an independent group that sends its own statement, sometimes weeks after the hospital bill.

You may never meet the doctor, which makes the bill feel like a mistake. It may not be one, but it deserves a check.

What should you compare it to?

Gather the hospital bill, the new statement and your EOBs, then match them up.

  • The date of service and the type of imaging or test listed.
  • Whether the same scan appears on both the hospital bill and the physician bill in a way that looks like double billing. A facility charge and a physician reading charge can be separate, but the same fee should not repeat.
  • What the EOB lists as your responsibility for this claim.
  • Whether the EOB shows the group as in-network or out-of-network.
  • Any item you do not recall, such as a second scan or an extra specimen.

What if the group is out-of-network?

For people with most private health plans, the No Surprises Act protects against balance billing by out-of-network providers at in-network facilities for non-emergency services, unless the patient gave proper notice and consent (42 U.S.C. § 300gg-112). Radiology and pathology at an in-network hospital are examples the law addresses.

For emergency care, a separate provision protects against balance billing by out-of-network emergency providers (42 U.S.C. § 300gg-111). Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE, VA and Indian Health Service coverage are outside this law.

How do you question the bill?

Call the group's billing office. Ask for an itemized statement, the date and type of the service, and whether they believe the No Surprises Act applies. Ask for a copy of any notice and consent you signed. Request a hold while it is reviewed.

Then call your insurer to confirm how it processed the claim and whether it should be reprocessed. Write down names, dates and reference numbers so that you can support a written dispute.

When should you escalate?

If you receive a bill you believe is not allowed, call the CMS No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059 or visit cms.gov/nosurprises. Your state insurance department handles insurer complaints, and your state attorney general may handle provider billing complaints.

Frequently asked questions

Is a separate radiologist bill normal?

Yes, it is common. Hospitals often bill for the facility while an independent radiology group bills for reading the images. It still deserves a check against your EOB. Look at the dates, the service and the amount, and confirm that the charge was processed according to your plan's network rules.

Am I being billed twice for the same scan?

Possibly, though a facility charge and a physician reading charge for the same scan are separate by design. It is a problem if the same item or fee appears twice, or if the amounts do not match your EOB. Request itemized statements from both and compare them.

Can I dispute a pathology bill for a biopsy?

Yes. Ask for an itemized statement showing each specimen billed, the date and the provider. Compare it with your EOB and your procedure records. If the pathology group is out-of-network at an in-network hospital, ask whether the No Surprises Act applies to the bill.

How do I know whether a radiologist was in my network?

Check your EOB, which often shows network status for each claim, or call your insurer with the provider's name and tax or billing information from the statement. A hospital being in-network does not mean every physician group working there is.

What if I never saw a radiologist or pathologist?

That is typical, since these doctors usually interpret results without meeting you. Not meeting them does not make the bill invalid. What matters is whether the service was actually provided on that date, whether the charge is accurate, and whether network rules were applied correctly to your claim.
