Disputing a Medical Bill in California: Which Regulator Is Yours?

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Dispute a bill by state
Short answerCalifornia has two health plan regulators. The Department of Managed Health Care oversees many HMO-style plans, and the California Department of Insurance oversees other health insurance. Your insurance card or plan documents usually show which one applies. The Attorney General handles consumer protection, and federal rules apply to everyone.

Find your regulator before you file

Unlike most states, California divides health plan oversight between two departments. Sending a complaint to the wrong one costs time, so identify yours first.

Look at your insurance card and member handbook for a regulator statement. If it names the Department of Managed Health Care, that office is your regulator. If it names the California Department of Insurance, go there. Still unsure? Call the member services number on your card and ask.

  • Department of Managed Health Care: oversees many HMO-style health plans.
  • California Department of Insurance: oversees other health insurance, such as many PPO-style policies.
  • Attorney General: consumer protection issues involving providers or collectors.
  • Self-funded employer plans, Medicare and Medicaid may follow different rules; confirm with the plan.

If you are in an HMO-style plan

Care in these plans often flows through medical groups and contracted providers, and bills can end up in the wrong place. A contracted provider billing you directly for a covered service is worth questioning with your plan first.

Ask member services whether the service was authorized and covered. Then ask the provider to bill the plan instead of you, and put that request in writing.

If you are in a PPO-style plan

Here the common problem is out-of-network billing. Compare your EOB with the provider's bill. If you were treated by an out-of-network anesthesiologist, radiologist or pathologist at an in-network hospital, the federal No Surprises Act generally protects you from balance billing for non-emergency services unless you gave proper notice and consent.

Federal rules that apply either way

These work in California regardless of regulator.

  • No Surprises Act: emergency care and covered out-of-network situations, with the exception of ground ambulances.
  • Fair Debt Collection Practices Act: dispute a collector's debt in writing within 30 days of the validation notice.
  • HIPAA right of access to medical and billing records, generally answered within 30 days.
  • Nonprofit hospital financial assistance policies under federal tax law.

Check California's own protections

State law may add rights, including for ground ambulance and health plan complaints. This page does not summarize them. Visit the regulator's website or call its help line to confirm what applies, then cite the specific protection in your letter.

Frequently asked questions

Is the DMHC or the Department of Insurance my regulator?

It depends on your plan type. Many HMO-style plans fall under the Department of Managed Health Care, while other health insurance falls under the California Department of Insurance. Check your insurance card or member handbook, or call the number on your card to confirm.

Can I complain to the regulator about a provider's bill?

Plan regulators focus on the health plan. If the issue is how a provider or collector is billing you, the Attorney General's consumer protection office may be a better fit. Many problems involve both, so describe the facts and ask where to file.

Do California ambulance bills fall under the No Surprises Act?

Air ambulance services are covered by the federal law, but ground ambulance services are not. Ask the state regulator what California protections apply to ground ambulance bills, then appeal to your insurer and negotiate if needed.

What if my California bill is from a collection agency?

Dispute it in writing within 30 days of receiving the validation notice. Under federal law the collector must stop collecting until it verifies the debt. Keep proof of mailing and consider a CFPB complaint if the collector does not comply.

How can I get my records to support a dispute?

Submit a written request to the provider under HIPAA. Providers must generally respond within 30 days. Ask for the itemized billing record and any records tied to the disputed date of service.
