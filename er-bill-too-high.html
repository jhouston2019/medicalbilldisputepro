Is your emergency room bill too high? What to check first

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Billing situations
Short answerAn emergency room bill is often several bills in one: a facility charge, a physician charge, and separate charges for tests and supplies. Request an itemized bill, compare it to your EOB, and check the visit level and any duplicate items. If you are insured, the No Surprises Act limits out-of-network emergency billing.

Why does one ER visit produce more than one bill?

An emergency visit usually involves several different billers. The hospital charges a facility fee for the room, staff and equipment. The emergency physician group often bills separately for the doctor's time. Radiology, pathology and lab work may add their own statements, sometimes weeks later. Because the bills arrive on different schedules, one visit can look like repeated charges when it is not.

Before disputing anything, list every statement you received for the visit and match each one to a line on your insurer's EOB. An EOB is not a bill, but its patient responsibility figure is your benchmark for what you may owe.

What should you look for on an emergency room bill?

Ask for an itemized bill rather than a summary, then read it line by line against the care you actually received.

  • The visit level. Emergency department visit codes run from 99281 to 99285, and a higher level means a higher charge, so ask what level was billed and why.
  • The same test, supply or medication listed twice on the same date.
  • Quantities that do not match what happened, such as several doses of a medication you received once.
  • A routine blood draw (venipuncture, code 36415) billed more than once for a single draw.
  • A wrong date of service, or services you do not remember receiving.
  • Amounts your insurer already paid that still show as owed.

Do the No Surprises Act protections apply to emergency care?

If you have most private health coverage, such as an employer or marketplace plan, the No Surprises Act protects you from balance billing by out-of-network emergency providers and facilities. It also generally limits your cost sharing to in-network levels (42 U.S.C. § 300gg-111).

It does not apply to Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE, VA or Indian Health Service coverage, which have their own rules. If you were charged more than your plan's in-network cost sharing after an emergency, compare the bill with your EOB and consider contacting the CMS No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059.

What should you say when you call the billing office?

Keep the call short and factual. Write down the date, the time, the name of the person and what they told you.

  • Ask for a fully itemized bill and confirm whether it comes from the hospital or from a physician group.
  • Ask them to explain the visit level and any line you do not recognize.
  • Ask whether the account can be put on hold while the charges are reviewed.
  • Ask for the dispute process in writing and for a reference number.
  • Ask whether your insurer was billed correctly and whether a corrected claim is needed.

When is a written dispute the better next step?

If a phone call does not settle it, write. A letter creates a dated record and lets you list each disputed line, the amount, and the result you want. Many providers and collectors expect disputes within a short window printed on the bill, so act promptly and keep proof of when you wrote.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my ER bill so much higher than my insurance estimate?

An estimate often reflects only what your plan expects to pay under typical terms, while the bill may combine charges from several providers. Compare the bill to your EOB. The patient responsibility amount on the EOB is what your plan says you owe, so anything above it deserves a question, especially if an out-of-network provider treated you in an emergency.

Can I dispute an ER bill if I already paid part of it?

Yes. Paying part of a bill does not usually stop you from questioning the rest, but ask the provider in writing whether a payment affects your dispute. Keep receipts, note the date, and make clear in your letter which specific charges you are disputing and which amounts you consider correct.

Is the ER facility fee negotiable?

Sometimes. Providers can adjust charges, offer payment plans or apply financial assistance. If the hospital is a nonprofit, it must have a written financial assistance policy under IRS rules, and you can usually find it on the hospital's website. Ask the billing office whether you qualify before you pay in full.

Who can help if the hospital will not fix an error?

Complaints about an insurer go to your state insurance department, and complaints about provider billing practices may go to your state attorney general. For possible No Surprises Act violations, the CMS No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059 can explain your options. Keep copies of your bills, EOBs and letters.

Does a higher ER visit level always mean upcoding?

No. A higher level can be appropriate when the care was complex. It becomes worth questioning when the level seems out of line with what happened, for example a short visit for a minor problem billed at the top level. Ask the provider to explain the level and request your records if you want to check.
