Disputing a Medical Bill in Ohio, Especially When Collectors Call

Last reviewed 2026-11-02 · Dispute a bill by state

If a collector has contacted you

Collection calls create pressure to pay quickly. Slow down. Under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, a collector must send a validation notice, and a consumer who disputes the debt in writing within 30 days of receiving it can require the collector to stop collection until the debt is verified.

This applies to third-party debt collectors, and generally not to the original provider collecting its own bill. Check who is actually contacting you.

What to put in a validation dispute

Keep the letter short and factual. Send it by a trackable method and keep a copy.

  • Your name, the collector's name and the account number on its notice.
  • A clear statement that you dispute the debt and request verification.
  • The date you received the validation notice.
  • A request that the collector stop collection until it verifies the debt.

Then go to the source of the bill

A collector is usually the last step in a chain that started with the provider and your insurer. Ask the provider for an itemized bill and compare it with your EOB. If the insurer should have paid more, the Ohio Department of Insurance takes complaints about insurers; check its website for how to file.

For unfair or deceptive collection or billing practices, the Ohio Attorney General's consumer protection office is another route. The CFPB also accepts complaints about debt collectors and credit reporting.

What not to do

Do not assume a collection account is accurate, and do not ignore it either. Avoid paying a partial amount on the phone without understanding how it will be recorded. Do not share bank details with a caller you cannot verify.

On credit reports, policies on medical collections have changed over time, so check current rules at consumerfinance.gov rather than relying on what someone tells you.

Ohio-specific protections

State law may add rules for collectors and for how long a debt can be pursued. This page does not describe them, and time limits vary by state. Review the Attorney General and Department of Insurance websites before relying on any state deadline.

Step-by-step

  1. Note the date you got the noticeThe 30-day dispute window runs from receipt of the validation notice, so write the date down immediately.
  2. Send a written disputeUse trackable mail or another method that proves delivery, and keep a copy.
  3. Contact the providerAsk for an itemized bill and a hold on the account while you review it against your EOB.
  4. Complain if rules are ignoredIf the collector keeps contacting you without verifying the debt, submit a CFPB complaint with your documents attached.

Frequently asked questions

How do I stop a medical debt collector in Ohio?

You generally cannot make a collector vanish, but a written dispute within 30 days of the validation notice requires it to stop collecting until it verifies the debt. Send it by trackable mail, keep a copy, and do not rely on phone conversations alone.

Does the validation rule apply if the hospital itself calls?

The federal debt collection law generally applies to third-party collectors, not the original provider collecting its own bill. A hospital billing office may still pause an account if you dispute the charges in writing, so ask directly.

Who regulates insurance in Ohio?

The Ohio Department of Insurance takes consumer complaints about insurers. If your employer funds its own plan, the state regulator may not have authority, so check your plan documents or ask HR which appeal route applies.

Can I complain about a collector in Ohio?

Yes. You can submit a complaint about a debt collector to the CFPB, and the Ohio Attorney General's consumer protection office may take complaints about unfair or deceptive practices. Include copies of your notice and dispute letter.

What is the statute of limitations on medical debt in Ohio?

It varies by state and by type of debt, and this page does not state a number. Check with the Attorney General's office or a consumer attorney before assuming a debt is too old to collect, since paying or acknowledging it may affect your position.
